Why Trust Can't Be Automated

In highly regulated industries like MedTech and Pharma, AI can accelerate documentation drafting, but regulatory bodies — including the FDA, EU Notified Bodies, and national competent authorities — require qualified human experts to verify and sign off on critical submissions. A 510(k) submission needs a regulatory specialist's review. A Clinical Evaluation Report requires clinical data appraisal by an experienced assessor. An ISO 13485 gap analysis demands a qualified auditor.

AI adoption is accelerating fast. CSO data shows Irish enterprise AI adoption nearly doubled in a single year, rising from 8% to 14.9% between 2023 and 2024, and the Government's National AI Strategy targets 75% of enterprises using cloud, AI and data analytics by 2030. Ibec's 2026 medtech research found that 97% of Irish manufacturing leaders in medtech, pharma and engineering plan to adopt AI to improve efficiency and productivity. As AI generates more regulatory content, faster, the bottleneck shifts — from drafting to qualified human verification.

The Regulatory Verification Gap

Every regulatory submission in life sciences follows the same pattern: documentation is prepared, then independently verified by qualified experts before it reaches a regulatory authority. This isn't optional — it's embedded in the regulatory frameworks themselves:

  • FDA 510(k) Predicate device comparison, performance data review, and biocompatibility assessment by specialists who understand classification rules and substantially equivalent determinations.
  • EU MDR CERs Clinical Evaluation Reports must be appraised by evaluators with relevant clinical and regulatory expertise, as defined in Article 61 and MDCG guidance (MDCG 2020-13).
  • ISO 13485 QMS Internal audits require trained auditors who are independent of the processes being audited (Clause 8.2.4).
  • 21 CFR Part 11 The review environment itself must meet regulatory standards — validated electronic record systems with complete audit trails.

None of these verification steps can be performed by AI alone. Each requires a qualified human expert with the right credentials, the right experience, and — critically — documented independence from the team that produced the work.

The problem is not a lack of experts — it's the process of engaging them. Companies spend 3–6 months on vendor qualification, QMS onboarding, non-disclosure agreement (NDA) negotiations, and contractual setup before a single document gets reviewed. How Expert Engagement Works Today

For most life sciences companies, engaging an external regulatory expert follows a process designed for long-term consulting relationships, not focused document verification:

  1. Identify a candidate — through personal networks, LinkedIn searches, or recruiter referrals. No centralised, pre-vetted marketplace exists for regulatory expertise.
  2. Vendor qualification — the expert must be onboarded into the company's approved supplier list, requiring quality team review, supplier questionnaires, and management sign-off.
  3. QMS onboarding — NDAs, confidentiality agreements, data processing agreements, and often company-specific QMS training before any work begins.
  4. Contractual negotiation — scope of work, payment terms, liability clauses, and IP assignment, each negotiated individually per engagement.
  5. Document exchange — files sent via email, shared drives, or ad hoc tools with no audit trail, no version control, and no automated retention policy.

This process takes 3–6 months per expert, per client. If a company needs three different specialists for a single submission — a common scenario for a 510(k) with biocompatibility, software, and sterility components — the overhead multiplies accordingly.
